About me

Kevin Keithley

I study systems whose behavior is richer than the simple story—then try to build a clearer model and something useful from it.

How I work

My career has moved through economic modeling, data science, hands-on business operations, and structural integration. The settings are different, but the technical impulse has remained constant: observe carefully, question the stock explanation, identify the important relationships and constraints, and test a better model against the real world.

At the International Monetary Fund, I supported global economic analysis, built and maintained modeling workflows, and automated data processes. I later earned an M.S. in Data Science from Tufts University, deepening my work in statistics, machine learning, databases, and software development.

I have also built businesses and systems in the physical world. I help manage catering operations producing more than $250,000 in annual revenue, where I work in the kitchen, execute events, and use software and process design to reduce friction and improve execution.

The projects I build now bring those parts of my background together: learning a system deeply, connecting ideas that usually remain separate, and turning that understanding into something other people can use.

A technical inquiry into movement

My move into structural integration was not a departure from technical work. I first encountered the work as a recipient and perceived something materially different from other approaches I had experienced—something the usual explanations did not adequately describe. I changed careers because I wanted to investigate it directly.

Through Body Transcendence, I spent approximately four years practicing, observing, and thinking closely about human movement and manual intervention. The practice earned unusual real-world validation, including approximately four years working with NHL player T.J. Oshie. That experience does not prove a theory, but it showed that the practical understanding I was developing could be valuable at an elite level.

I am now making that long-running technical inquiry explicit. I am exploring human movement as a state-dependent system shaped by constraints, redundancy, passive structure, and control—not merely by visible posture or isolated muscle strength. Physical examples such as a Jacob's ladder help clarify how the same components can have very different available movements when their internal constraint organization changes: movement can be restored by reorganizing relationships, without adding strength or changing the material itself.

This is a developing model, not a finished biological claim. The goal is to translate high-resolution practical observations into hypotheses that can be stated clearly, modeled mechanically, and eventually investigated with measurement—including motion, 3D, and spatial data.
